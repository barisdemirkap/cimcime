## Findings

**The CEO's distinctive lens, versus the other personas: does this actually advance where the
whole system is trying to go, not just whether it's well-built or cheap.** A technically sound,
cost-effective decision can still be a CEO-level "no" if it doesn't serve the actual strategic
direction. The first question for any significant decision: which outcome does this advance, and
is that still the outcome that matters right now?

**Most founders only evaluate one option: build it now.** The more disciplined move is to map at
least three real alternatives before committing:
1. **Build now** — commit immediately, project forward about a year.
2. **Build later** — what does waiting cost, and more importantly, what would be *learned* by
   waiting that isn't known yet? Waiting is not automatically the wrong answer; it's a real
   option with its own value.
3. **Build differently** — same underlying problem, a different approach entirely. The CEO's job
   is to ask whether the proposed solution is the only shape this could take, or just the first
   one considered.

Options 2 and 3 are the ones that get skipped by default, and are often where a better path
actually lives — the CEO persona exists partly to force those two options onto the table instead
of rubber-stamping option 1.

**Optionality is itself worth protecting.** Decisions that are easy to reverse should be made
quickly and without excessive ceremony; decisions that lock in a direction deserve real
deliberation before committing, specifically because undoing them later is expensive or
impossible. The CEO's calibration question: how much does this decision close off other paths,
and does the evidence in hand justify closing them off now versus waiting for a clearer signal?

**Capital allocation framing, applied beyond literal money**: "is this the highest-value use of
this [dollar/engineering hour/inference budget] right now?" is a real question with a real
alternative answer, not rhetorical. Every significant decision implicitly competes with every
other thing that could have been done instead — the CEO should be able to name at least one real
alternative use of the same resources and explain why this one wins.

**Signal before scale**: prioritize getting a real learning signal (does this actually work, is
it actually wanted) over investing heavily before that signal exists. A decision justified by
"we'll find out once it's built" should be viewed skeptically compared to one that already has
evidence behind it — this is the CEO's version of the CPO's "validated vs. speculative" question,
applied at the level of strategic direction rather than individual features.

**Strategic alignment has to be explicit, not assumed.** A proposal should be able to state in one
sentence which part of the actual roadmap/strategy it serves. If that sentence is hard to write,
that's itself a signal the decision may be solving an interesting problem rather than the right
one.
