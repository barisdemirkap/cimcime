## Findings

**Origin**: Palantir created the FDE model in the early 2010s out of necessity — intelligence
agency customers couldn't openly disclose their real needs through normal product-discovery
conversations, so Palantir embedded engineers directly in the customer's environment to learn by
observing and building in real time, rather than by being told requirements upfront.

**"Echo" and "Delta" teams — the actual origin of this topic's name**: Palantir's model pairs two
complementary team types. **Echo teams** are domain experts from the client's own industry who
identify high-value problems and translate between the customer's and the engineering team's
perspectives. **Delta teams** are the execution-focused engineers who build fast, prioritizing
speed and impact while working from incomplete information — the actual "forward deployed
engineers" in the narrowest sense. Together an Echo+Delta pairing functions like a mini-startup
embedded inside each client.

**"Gravel road to paved highway"**: the mechanism that distinguishes an FDE from a consultant.
Custom field solutions built for one client ("gravel roads") get recognized as recurring patterns,
which core engineering then standardizes into real product features ("paved highways"). The
FDE's one-off work is explicitly meant to feed back into a scalable product, not just solve one
client's problem in isolation — directly echoing `introduction.md`'s finding about FDEs feeding
field learnings back to product teams.

**Why AI companies specifically are re-adopting this model now**: AI systems hit the same
category of problem Palantir hit — undefined customer needs, highly variable deployment
environments, and solutions that work in research but break in production. The source names
OpenAI and Ramp as companies that have already stood up FDE teams for exactly this reason: "no
single solution works everywhere" in AI deployment.
